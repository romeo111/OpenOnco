Genesis opens potency data; Ruby has not shown a patient benefit

The public dataset lists 5,410,616 records with source provenance. Ruby benchmarks address laboratory prediction, not cancer treatment outcomes.

by OpenOnco

Written retrospectively and published on November 23, 2026.

A company announcement is not independent evidence of clinical benefit. This comparison is informational, not medical advice. This article does not replace a doctor.

On 20 November 2026, Genesis Molecular AI announced Ruby, a model for predicting how strongly small molecules act on protein targets, and released part of its training data. The public GenesisPotency-2026 dataset is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0 and links measurements back to ChEMBL, BindingDB or PubChem records. Its README counts 5,410,616 rows, whereas the launch blog says 5.6 million. The dataset is free to download; the repository does not provide Ruby model weights or a public price for Ruby.

Genesis reports that Ruby reached an average Spearman correlation of 0.61 across eight assays from five Genesis–Incyte drug-discovery programs without prior measurements from those assays. This is the company's own evaluation, alongside public benchmarks, not an independent clinical trial. Predicting molecular potency can help researchers choose compounds to test; it does not establish that a medicine treats cancer in people. The dataset README explicitly says it is not for clinical or diagnostic decisions.

OpenOnco is free to read and publishes MIT-licensed code, inspectable rules and cited oncology knowledge for synthetic case drafts. It does not predict protein–ligand potency or develop new medicines. Genesis's public assay data and OpenOnco's source-linked clinical knowledge serve different stages of research and care; neither has a direct accuracy or patient-outcome comparison with the other. Sources checked 23 November 2026. This article does not replace a doctor.
